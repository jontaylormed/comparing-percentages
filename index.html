Step 1 · Meet the Data

254,278 real people.
Who are they?

Before any math — let's meet the people. The U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey estimates that 254,278 people born outside the U.S. lived in the Seattle CCD (a Census area that includes the city of Seattle) during 2017–2021. It's an estimate from a survey, not a count of every person. The Census splits them into three groups by when they came to live in the U.S.:

Group A · Arrived 2010 or later

91,815 people. Newest arrivals. 59.3% are ages 25 to 44. The Census table doesn't record why people came.

Group B · Arrived 2000–2009

61,921 people. By 2021 they had lived in the U.S. about 12 to 21 years. 75.8% of those 16 and older have jobs. This is the smallest group.

Group C · Arrived before 2000

100,542 people. By 2021 they had lived in the U.S. more than 21 years. Most are now U.S. citizens (79.0%), and 60.7% of their households own their home.

🌏 Where are they from? Asia is the most common birthplace region in every group (41.1% to 57.2%). But the mix changes over time — Africa's share more than doubled from Group C (6.5%) to Group A (15.5%), and Latin America's share is biggest in Group B (26.3%). How can Group A have the highest income AND the highest poverty rate? +
Group A has the highest median household income estimate ($95,809) and also the highest poverty rate (18.4%). The group looks split: 54.2% of its full-time workers earn $75,000 or more, yet 18.4% of its people live below the poverty line. One number can't show both sides. We'll learn why in this lesson.
Group A is the youngest — so why doesn't it have the most workers? +
Group B has the highest share in the labor force, meaning working or looking for work (79.2%), vs Group A (73.4%). Group A has the most people enrolled in school (24,793, vs 8,507 in Group B). Group B's median age is 38.9, prime working age. Being young doesn't always mean working more.
Groups A and C are close in size — do they have about the same number of homeowners? +
No! Homeownership is counted by household, not by person. Group A has 33,946 households, and 22.2% own their home ≈ 7,536. Group C has 52,504 households, and 60.7% own ≈ 31,870. (The Census table prints the exact counts: 7,540 and 31,845.) Group C has about 4 times as many homeowner households, though it has only about 1.1 times as many people. The rate (%) and the right whole both matter.

Notice the groups are different sizes. This is the first thing to check when comparing percentages — the same percentage means a different number of people depending on how big the group is.

Part 1 · What Is a Percentage?

What does a percentage actually mean?

📖 Definition: Percentage A percentage tells you how many out of every 100. It lets you compare groups of different sizes fairly.
Percentage = (Part ÷ Whole) × 100 What fraction of the total does this part represent?

Try it: 57.2% of Group A were born in Asia. Group A has 91,815 people. How many is that?

91,815 × 0.572 ≈ 52,518 people from Asia
🔑 Key idea Same % + bigger group = more actual people.
57% of 100,000 is way more people than 57% of 50,000.
Groups A & C are both ~57% Asian-born — but different actual counts

Part 2 · Comparing Percentages

3 ways to compare percentages

1️⃣ Which is bigger?
Just look at the numbers. Which is larger?
Example: 34.8% vs 15.9% → Group A is bigger.
2️⃣ Percentage point difference — subtract
📖 Percentage point = the gap between two percentages.
Example: 34.8 − 15.9 = 18.9 percentage points.
3️⃣ Ratio — divide
📖 Ratio = how many times bigger one number is than another.
Example: 34.8 ÷ 15.9 ≈ 2.2× — Group A's rate is more than double Group C's.

Part 3 · Watch Out!

3 common mistakes

⚠️ Mistake 1 · Same % doesn't mean same count

Groups A and C are both ~57% Asian-born. Same percentage — but Group C is bigger (~100k vs ~92k people), so it actually has more Asian-born residents. Equal percentages ≠ equal numbers.

⚠️ Mistake 2 · Percentage points vs. ratio — don't mix them up

Group A: 34.8% grad degrees. Group C: 15.9%. Subtract → 18.9 percentage point gap. Divide → Group A is 2.2× bigger. These are two different facts. Make sure you know which one you're using.

⚠️ Mistake 3 · One number hides the full picture

Group A has the highest median income estimate ($95,809) AND the highest poverty rate (18.4%). How? Many of its full-time workers earn a lot (54.2% earn $75,000 or more), while 18.4% of its people live in poverty. One percentage can't show both sides of a group. (And check the margin of error: ±$11,184 on that income means it may not truly be higher than Group C's.)

Group A: highest median income estimate AND highest poverty — how?
Rule to remember One number is never the whole story. Always look at a few different facts before making a conclusion. Ask yourself: what question am I actually trying to answer?
